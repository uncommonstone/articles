The Calm Before the Storm

For three decades, India's information technology industry has been the single most reliable engine of national prosperity. From Bangalore's glass-and-steel campuses to Hyderabad's Cyberabad corridor and Pune's Hinjewadi tech park, the sector has lifted millions into the middle class, stabilised the rupee through vast dollar inflows, and anchored urban real estate markets from Chennai to Delhi-NCR. In FY2025, the industry generated $283 billion in revenue—representing 7.3% of India's GDP—and employed 5.8 million professionals directly, with an indirect employment multiplier of approximately 3× suggesting a total ecosystem of 17–20 million livelihoods.

That edifice is now under existential pressure. Generative AI, agentic coding tools, and large language model–powered automation are compressing the labour content of software development, testing, IT support, business process management, and entry-level consulting at a pace that far outstrips normal technological disruption. This report models the macroeconomic consequences of a 20% to 60% reduction in India's tech workforce over the seven-year window 2026–2032—a range that reflects the wide uncertainty surrounding both the pace of AI adoption and the capacity of the Indian economy to absorb or redirect displaced workers.

Methodology & Assumptions. GDP baseline: $4.15 trillion (projected FY26) at 6.5% baseline annual growth. Analysis window: 2026–2032 (7 years). Direct IT employment base: 5.8 million. India's Tech Sector: A Foundation Carved in Silicon

Before quantifying the damage, it is worth appreciating just how deeply the technology sector has been woven into the fabric of modern India. The industry's $224 billion in annual IT services exports constitutes 43–45% of India's total services exports and is the single largest contributor to the country's foreign exchange reserves—a structural prop for the rupee that no other sector comes close to matching. NASSCOM projects this could grow to $350 billion by 2026 and $1 trillion by 2030 under a business-as-usual scenario. Those projections must now be viewed with considerable scepticism.

The sector's "Big Five"—TCS, Infosys, HCLTech, Wipro, and Tech Mahindra—together employ approximately 1.4 million workers and generate roughly $80 billion in combined annual revenue. Their campuses dominate the commercial real estate markets of Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai, and Pune. Their employees account for a disproportionate share of premium automobile purchases, housing loans, consumer electronics spend, and domestic aviation traffic in those cities.

Chart 1: The Employment Cliff

The most immediate and visible impact of AI-driven displacement will be on employment. India's IT workforce is heavily concentrated in a narrow band of roles: software development and testing (the largest), IT support and operations, data analytics, business process outsourcing, and mid-level project management. These are precisely the functions where AI agents, LLM-powered code generation, and automated testing pipelines are achieving the most dramatic productivity gains.

Under the Moderate scenario (20% job loss), approximately 1.16 million direct IT jobs disappear over seven years—equivalent to the entire combined workforce of Wipro and Tech Mahindra. Applying the 3× indirect employment multiplier, total economy-wide job losses reach approximately 4.6 million. These are positions in food delivery services, commercial real estate maintenance, retail, domestic services, and informal construction—all dependent on the spending power of IT workers concentrated in six major cities.

Why 3×? The multiplier captures two distinct downstream effects. The first is the spending effect: an IT professional earning ₹12–20 lakh per year sustains a web of informal livelihoods through rent, domestic help, food delivery, transport, and retail — when that income disappears, so do those jobs. The second is the supply chain effect: every IT campus requires facility management crews, cafeteria staff, security personnel, cab aggregator drivers, and hardware subcontractors who sit outside the official 5.8 million headcount but exist entirely because the sector does. NASSCOM and RBI research has cited indirect multipliers of 3–4× for India's IT-BPM sector specifically, reflecting the above-average salaries of IT workers (which amplify the spending effect) and the dense informal economy that has built up around IT corridors in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, and Pune. A more conservative 2× multiplier would yield ~3.5 million total economy losses in the moderate scenario rather than the ~4.6 million shown at 3× — and ~10.4 million rather than ~13.9 million in the severe scenario. At 3×, the indirect losses are three times the direct figure, meaning the visible layoff count understates the true economic displacement by a factor of four. Readers should treat the multiplier as directionally sound rather than precisely calibrated.

Under the Severe scenario (60% job loss), the numbers are staggering: 3.48 million direct IT positions eliminated, with total economy-wide displacement reaching nearly 14 million. To place this in context, that would exceed the total workforce of the entire Indian manufacturing sector's formal employment in a single seven-year window.

The generational dimension of this disruption deserves particular attention. India produces approximately 1.5 million engineering graduates per year, a significant share of whom enter the IT sector. AI displacement will not merely eliminate existing jobs—it will shrink the funnel for the hundreds of thousands of young Indians who would have joined the industry each year. Retraining pathways are narrower than policymakers typically assume: the same AI tools eliminating junior developer roles will also eliminate the mid-level roles that junior developers would have graduated into.

"Each IT professional laid off carries with them not just a salary, but a rent payment, a car EMI, a school fee, and the livelihoods of three to four others in the informal economy who depended on their spending."

Chart 2: The GDP Drag

India's GDP, currently approximately $4.15 trillion heading into 2026, has benefited enormously from the IT sector's dollar earnings, domestic consumption, and multiplier effects through the broader economy. The IT industry contributes directly to national income through employee wages, corporate profits, and tax revenues, and indirectly through its stimulation of real estate development, consumer goods demand, and financial services activity.

Under the Moderate scenario, we model a reduction in India's annual GDP growth rate of approximately 0.5–0.7 percentage points from a baseline of 6.5%, as direct IT output contracts and the consumer spending drag filters through the economy. Over the full seven-year window, this would lower India's projected 2032 GDP from $6.06 trillion (baseline) to approximately $5.81 trillion—a terminal-year shortfall of roughly $250 billion.

Under the Severe scenario, the impact compounds significantly over the seven-year horizon. Growth slows by approximately 2.0–2.5 percentage points annually, dragging India's 2032 GDP toward approximately $5.16 trillion against the $6.06 trillion baseline. The cumulative GDP loss relative to baseline approaches $500–600 billion over the seven-year period. To appreciate the magnitude: that is roughly equivalent to erasing the entire economy of Vietnam or the Philippines — two of Southeast Asia's most dynamic emerging markets.

Importantly, the indirect channels amplify the direct IT revenue loss. Consumer spending by 5.8 million IT workers—who earn 3–5× the national average wage—accounts for a disproportionate share of premium retail, premium housing, new vehicle purchases, and domestic leisure travel. The contraction of this spending power will ripple through those adjacent sectors in ways that compound the headline GDP impact.

Chart 3: The Rupee Under Pressure

No aspect of the IT sector's macroeconomic role is more underappreciated than its role as India's de facto forex reserve generator. IT services exports of $224 billion annually—flowing primarily from US and European clients to Indian vendors—are the structural backstop of the rupee's managed float against the dollar. The RBI's ability to manage currency volatility depends in no small part on this steady, predictable dollar inflow.

The rupee's vulnerability is not hypothetical — it is already visible in real-time data. As of April 2, 2026, the USD/INR rate stands at ₹93.2, having depreciated approximately 9.3% over the prior twelve months amid persistent capital outflows and geopolitical pressure on emerging market currencies. This means the starting point for our disruption scenario is already materially weaker than consensus forecasts from just a year ago assumed — and any further structural reduction in IT export inflows compounds a pre-existing weakness.

A 20% reduction in IT employment, assuming proportional contraction in exports, removes approximately $45 billion in annual dollar inflows. Over seven years, this represents a cumulative forex shortfall of $90–150 billion — enough to materially strain India's current account. In this scenario, we model the INR weakening from its current ₹93.2 to approximately ₹107–108 per dollar by 2032, absent countervailing measures.

The severe scenario is considerably more alarming. A 60% workforce reduction, applied proportionally to exports, would remove roughly $134 billion from annual forex earnings. Combined with the knock-on effects of reduced FDI confidence and slower service-sector growth, our model suggests the rupee could weaken toward ₹122–123 per dollar by 2032. Every 1% depreciation of the rupee increases the cost of India's energy imports (denominated in dollars) and raises inflation — creating a secondary economic feedback loop that further suppresses domestic consumption.

It is worth noting that rupee depreciation has traditionally been a partial offset for IT companies, since their dollar revenues become more valuable in rupee terms. But in a scenario where revenue volumes themselves are falling—as clients deploy AI to reduce their outsourcing requirements—this exchange rate cushion diminishes substantially.

Chart 4: Real Estate — The Hidden Casualty

India's IT sector accounts for 33% of Grade-A commercial office leasing across the top-six cities, and Bengaluru and Hyderabad together represent roughly half of all tech-related leasing activity in the country. The residential real estate markets of Electronic City, Whitefield, HITEC City, Gachibowli, and Hinjewadi are fundamentally driven by the income and confidence of IT professionals.

The commercial real estate impact will arrive first and most visibly. Office vacancy rates in India's top IT cities currently hover around 14–16%. A significant reduction in tech employment translates directly into lease non-renewals, subletting, and a collapse in new pre-lease commitments from IT firms. Under the moderate scenario, vacancy rates in IT-heavy submarkets could climb to 30–34% by 2031–2032, as lease non-renewals accumulate over the period. Under the severe scenario, vacancies could reach ~60% by 2032—a level comparable to the worst post-GFC markets globally and sufficient to trigger widespread developer defaults.

Residential prices in IT corridors have appreciated 15–25% annually in cities like Bengaluru and Hyderabad over the past three years, driven by demand from IT employees flush with stock options and performance bonuses. This dynamic reverses sharply when the income base contracts. In the moderate scenario, we model residential price indices in these corridors falling approximately ~18% from peak by 2032 before gradually stabilising. In the severe scenario, a ~48% drawdown from peak values is plausible by 2032—with particular severity in peripheral micro-markets where speculative investor activity has been highest.

The implications extend beyond direct real estate losses. Indian banks hold substantial mortgage and construction finance exposure to IT-corridor residential and commercial properties. A 30–40% price correction in these micro-markets would generate non-performing asset pressures at several large private and public sector banks—particularly those with heavy Bengaluru and Hyderabad exposure. The RBI would face the unenviable challenge of simultaneously managing a weaker currency, slower growth, and rising bank stress.

Consumer Discretionary Spending & Auto Sales

India's passenger vehicle market sells approximately 4.2–4.5 million units annually. The premium and mid-premium segments (vehicles priced above ₹15 lakh) are heavily concentrated among IT professionals, particularly in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, and Chennai. A meaningful contraction in IT employment would remove a significant proportion of the creditworthy, aspirational buyers who have driven this market's growth.

Under the moderate scenario, annual passenger vehicle sales in IT-heavy cities could decline 8–15% relative to baseline, with the mid-premium and premium segments contracting 20–30%. Discretionary retail—electronics, premium apparel, dining, and domestic travel—faces proportional headwinds. Under the severe scenario, the overall Indian passenger vehicle market could contract by 12–20% from peak demand, representing a reversal of nearly ten years of volume growth.

The consumer credit implications are significant. Banks and NBFCs with large personal loan, auto loan, and credit card portfolios concentrated among IT professionals would see delinquency rates rise as EMI burdens become unsustainable for displaced workers whose re-employment prospects are uncertain and likely to be at substantially lower compensation levels.

Regional Concentration Risk

Unlike many economic shocks that spread broadly across geography and sectors, an IT employment contraction would be geographically concentrated in a handful of urban agglomerations. Bengaluru—a city whose GDP per capita and real estate market are essentially underwritten by the IT sector—faces the most severe exposure. By some estimates, 35–40% of Bengaluru's formal sector employment is either directly in IT or immediately dependent on IT worker spending. Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, and parts of Delhi-NCR face similar structural vulnerabilities.

This geographic concentration creates the risk of urban economic feedback spirals: falling property prices reduce household wealth and consumer confidence among even those who retain their jobs; falling local government tax revenues reduce city services; and declining business confidence suppresses new investment, creating a self-reinforcing contraction that is difficult to arrest without significant federal fiscal intervention.

The Counterargument: Reinvention and Resilience

Any honest analysis must acknowledge the countervailing forces. India's demographic dividend—a young, English-speaking, technically literate population—positions it better than most countries to adapt to AI-driven economic change. The same AI boom driving displacement is also creating new categories of employment: AI trainers, model evaluators, prompt engineers, AI-infrastructure builders, and the vast ecosystem of businesses that will emerge to deploy AI across India's domestic economy.

India's large domestic market—increasingly digitised—will create new IT demand that partially offsets the loss of export-oriented outsourcing work. Global Capability Centres (GCCs) of multinational companies, which now employ over 1.6 million in India, are expanding rather than contracting their India footprint, as they recognise India's AI talent advantage. The government's Digital India initiatives and significant investments in semiconductor manufacturing create potential new employment vectors.

However, the honest caveat is this: the speed of displacement will almost certainly outpace the speed of reinvention. Retraining a 40-year-old Java developer for AI-era roles is a 2–4 year process, not a 6-month one. And the new AI-era jobs being created are fewer in number than the jobs being automated away—at least in the near term.

Policy Imperatives

The scale of the potential disruption demands proactive policy responses that are not yet visible from either the central government or state administrations. Among the most urgent priorities:

Extended retraining programmes with genuine income replacement during transition periods—not the nominal ₹1,500/month currently offered under most central government skill development schemes. Workers earning ₹12–25 lakh per year cannot absorb six months of retraining on a subsistence stipend.

Urban economic diversification strategies for Bengaluru, Hyderabad, and Pune—cities that have systematically allowed manufacturing, creative industries, and non-IT services to atrophy in favour of tech-sector land and labour market optimisation. The monoculture risk is now apparent.

Tax incentive restructuring to attract AI-era investment—data centres, model training infrastructure, and semiconductor fabrication—to India, creating domestic capital investment that generates employment less susceptible to the specific automation risk facing services outsourcing.

Rupee management frameworks that anticipate structurally lower IT export inflows and build compensating mechanisms—diversifying India's export basket toward green energy, defence, pharmaceuticals, and advanced manufacturing.

Conclusion: A Reckoning Long in the Making

India's IT sector success was, in many ways, a product of a specific and time-limited arbitrage: cheap, abundant, English-speaking engineering talent at a time when the world needed enormous amounts of relatively routine software development and IT services work. AI is not just the next automation wave—it is the technology that most directly automates the specific type of cognitive labour that Indian IT has specialised in supplying.

The 20% scenario is uncomfortable. The 60% scenario is generationally disruptive. Both are plausible within the seven-year window, and the truth will likely lie somewhere in between—with significant variance across subsectors (BPO and testing hardest hit, strategic consulting and R&D more resilient), across firm types (large incumbents with AI investment capacity versus smaller tier-2 firms), and across individual skill profiles.

What is not in doubt is that the Indian economy's most important sector faces its most significant structural challenge since the Y2K boom launched its modern era. How India—its government, its businesses, and its millions of tech workers—responds to that challenge over the next seven years will do more to shape the nation's economic trajectory than any other variable currently in play.

The data demands urgency. The policy window is now.
