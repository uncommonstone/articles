Where India's
Dollars Go

India earns foreign currency on a vast scale — and spends nearly all of it. The composition of that spending is the real story of an emerging economy.

Anatomy of an import bill ≈ 8 min read

For every billion dollars that arrives in India through exports, remittances, or foreign investment, almost the same amount flows back out again. The country runs one of the largest gross external accounts in the world, but the residual — what actually adds to reserves at year-end — is a thin sliver on top of a colossal two-way churn.

That churn is not a sign of trouble. It is the financial signature of an economy that imports the energy, the components, and the capital goods it needs to produce, then exports services and finished products back. The interesting question is not whether the money leaves — it must — but where, exactly, it goes.

The two diagrams below answer that question at two different magnifications. The first traces the full circuit. The second zooms into the single largest outflow channel: the import bill itself.

Three things jump out. Imports are the single biggest line. Debt servicing, repatriated profits, and outbound travel together are smaller than what the country pays for crude oil alone in a typical year. And the reserves stock at the centre — ~$700 billion — is the cushion the system has built up over decades of net positive inflows.

If imports dominate the outflow side, the natural follow-on question is: what is India importing? India's total import bill reached roughly $698 billion in 2024, up 3.8% year over year. The composition reveals the country's structural dependencies.

— The three lines that matterOil, electronics, gold

Three categories deserve close attention because each tells a different story about the Indian economy.

Crude oil is the inheritance. A single product — crude — accounted for roughly a fifth of all Indian imports in 2024, at $141 billion. Including coal, gas, and other fuels, the broader Minerals & Fuels category made up 33% of the entire import bill. India imports more than 85% of the crude it consumes. Every ten-dollar move in Brent translates to billions on the trade balance, and the rupee tends to move with it. This is the country's most enduring external vulnerability.

Electronics is the new story. Machinery and electronics together totalled $144 billion in 2024, with electronic integrated circuits ($23B), mobile phones ($17B), and computers ($10B) leading the list. Despite the "Make in India" push and the production-linked incentive schemes that have drawn smartphone assembly onshore, the components and the chips still come from abroad — increasingly from China, Taiwan, and South Korea. Assembling iPhones in Tamil Nadu does not, by itself, close the electronics gap; the value sits in the components.

"India imports the energy, the components, and the capital goods it needs to produce — then exports services and finished products back."

Gold is the cultural wildcard. Indian households hold an estimated 25,000 tonnes of gold — more than the official reserves of every major central bank combined. Wedding and festival demand is a fixed feature of every quarter. Gold imports surged 214% in a single recent month, driven by cultural demand and investment buying. The government periodically raises import duties to throttle the flow; it returns the moment duties ease. Unlike oil or chips, this outflow has nothing to do with production. It is wealth conversion: rupees in, metal out.

— Beyond importsThe other things India pays for

The merchandise bill is not the whole story. India also pays foreigners roughly $95 billion a year to service its external debt, sends about $45 billion abroad as repatriated profits from multinationals operating locally, and spends another $30 billion or so on overseas travel, foreign tuition, and software royalties under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme. Indian companies acquiring abroad add another $15 billion of outward direct investment.

These are all real outflows, but they are an order of magnitude smaller than goods imports. A single bad month for crude oil prices moves the headline figures more than an entire year of FPI exits or dividend repatriation. Which is why, when economists worry about India's external position, they almost always end up talking about energy.

— What this meansThe composition is the policy

The shape of an import bill is a country's strategic disclosure. India's says: we are an economy that runs on energy we don't produce, components we don't make, and a private appetite for gold that nothing has been able to suppress. Each of those is a policy target. Domestic refining and the strategic petroleum reserve attack the first. The PLI schemes for semiconductors and electronics attack the second. Gold monetisation schemes and sovereign gold bonds attack the third — with very mixed results.

None of these will move quickly. But they are the right places to look, because the import bill is where the bulk of India's foreign earnings actually go. Everything else — the FDI flows, the FPI swings, the remittance windfalls — is the fluctuation on top of this much heavier baseline.
